# Connecticut borough statutes and constitution (primary text), with Vermont village statutes

Research date: 17 November 2026. Unless marked otherwise, every quotation below was read directly from the primary page (downloaded with curl and read as plain text), not through a summarising tool. Items marked (snippet) come from a search-result summary only. The Connecticut statute pages are the General Assembly's "current" online General Statutes; each chapter page carries the note "Readers should refer to the 2026 Supplement, revised to January 1, 2026, for updated versions of statutes amended, repealed or added during the 2025 legislative sessions." The 2026 Supplement was not separately checked. Vermont sections are from Vermont Statutes Online (legislature.vermont.gov).

Relation to the earlier note (`state_law_and_practice.md`): these findings confirm its legal conclusions and close its two flagged gaps (Connecticut statute text; Vermont §§ 1303 and 1309). They add three qualifications the earlier note did not have: (1) Connecticut statute itself labels a borough a "political subdivision" of the town, in the same list as a "district"; (2) the property-assessment statutes define "municipality"/"town" so as to exclude unconsolidated boroughs, because the town does the assessing; (3) Groton Long Point is not a borough in state law but a special-act association (a special taxing district), so the Census "borough" label for it is a Census convention.

## 1. How Connecticut statutes define "municipality", and whether boroughs have the same enumerated powers as towns and cities

### Takeaway
The core municipal-powers and home-rule definitions name boroughs expressly and on equal terms with towns and cities (C.G.S. §§ 7-148(a), 7-187(d), 9-1(i)), so a borough has the full § 7-148(c) list of powers, including taxation, police, fire, streets and general police-power regulation. Boroughs are excluded only from definitions tied to functions the town performs for the whole town, chiefly property assessment and the grand list.

### Cited Findings
Definitions that include boroughs:
- C.G.S. § 7-148(a): "Whenever used in this section, 'municipality' means any town, city or borough, consolidated town and city or consolidated town and borough." — [C.G.S. ch. 98, § 7-148](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_098.htm#sec_7-148)
- C.G.S. § 7-148(c): "Any municipality shall have the power to do any of the following, in addition to all powers granted to municipalities under the Constitution and general statutes:" followed by the enumerated list, which includes "(2) Finances and appropriations. ... (B) Assess, levy and collect taxes for general or special purposes on all property, subjects or objects which may be lawfully taxed"; "(4) Public services. (A) Provide for police protection, regulate and prescribe the duties of the persons providing police protection with respect to criminal matters within the limits of the municipality ..."; "(B) Provide for fire protection ..."; "Lay out, construct, reconstruct, alter, maintain, repair, control, operate, and assign numbers to streets, alleys, highways ..."; and "Make and enforce police, sanitary or other similar regulations and protect or promote the peace, safety, good government and welfare of the municipality and its inhabitants". ### Inferences
- On the two statutes that matter most for "what kind of entity is it" (§ 7-148 municipal powers; ch. 99 home rule), a borough is a municipality with exactly the powers of a town or city. That is a stronger statutory position than a Connecticut special taxing district, whose powers are the closed list in § 7-326, and is at least as strong as a New York village's.
- The exclusions are functional: assessment, the grand list, revaluation and voting districts belong to the town, so those sections define "municipality" to mean the town-level unit. This parallels Michigan, where the township assesses for the village.
- The statutes sometimes group "borough" with towns and cities and sometimes with "district or association with municipal powers"; Connecticut law does not treat the line between a borough and a special-act district as sharp (see section 5).

### Gaps
- Not every one of the several hundred definitions of "municipality" in the General Statutes was surveyed; Titles 1, 7, 8, 9 and 12 were searched.

## 2. Which statutes govern borough government; creation by special act; consolidation and dissolution

### Takeaway
There is no "boroughs" chapter in the current General Statutes. Boroughs are creatures of special-act charters (now amendable under the home rule chapter), and the general statutes simply slot the warden and burgesses into provisions written for all municipalities. The consolidation statute (§§ 7-195 to 7-201) classifies a borough as a "political subdivision" of its town, alongside a city or district within a town, and consolidation is decided by a vote of the electors of the whole town.

### Cited Findings
- Title 7 ("Municipalities") chapter list: ch. 90 Town and Other Community Meetings; 91 Selectmen; 92 Town Clerks; 95 Constables; 96 Town Manager; 97 Municipalities: General Provisions; 98 Municipal Powers; 99 Municipal Charters and Special Acts; 104 Police and Fire Protection; 105 Fire, Sewer and Other Districts; 105a Municipal Special Services Districts; and so on. No chapter is devoted to boroughs or to cities. — [C.G.S. Title 7 contents](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/title_07.htm)
- Special-act origin, OLR Report 98-R-0660 ("History of Connecticut's Boroughs", 12 June 1998): "The legislature created the boroughs by special act on behalf of the people residing in those sections." "All of the state's eight boroughs operate under special act charters, which can be amended under the home rule statutes. New boroughs would also have to be established by special act, since the law does not allow residents to establish them on their own. The eight boroughs are Woodmont in Milford, Bantam and Litchfield in Litchfield, Danielson in Killingly, Fenwick in Old Saybrook, Jewett City in Griswold, Newtown in Newtown, and Stonington in Stonington." — [OLR 98-R-0660](https://www.cga.ct.gov/PS98/rpt/olr/htm/98-r-0660.htm)

### Inferences
- A borough can only come into being by special act of the General Assembly; it can end either by special act or by the general consolidation procedure in §§ 7-195 to 7-201, which requires a town-wide majority, not a borough majority. (The New York Village Law, by contrast, lets residents incorporate and dissolve a village by local petition and vote.) In this one respect a Connecticut borough is less self-determining than a New York village.
- Section 7-195's phrase "a town or political subdivision thereof" and its definition of "political subdivision" as "a city, borough or district within a town" is the clearest statutory statement that the borough is a subordinate part of the town. But the same sentence treats the City of Groton identically, and elsewhere (Art. Tenth § 1) towns, cities and boroughs are all called "political subdivisions" of the State.

### Gaps
- No general-statute procedure for dissolving a borough other than consolidation under §§ 7-195 to 7-201 was found; a text search of Titles 1, 7, 8, 9 and 12 for borough dissolution or disincorporation returned nothing. Whether past disincorporations (Colchester 1990, Stafford Springs 1991) used § 7-195 or special acts was not researched.
- Individual borough charters (special acts) were not read.

## 3. Connecticut Constitution, Article Tenth (home rule): does it name boroughs?

### Takeaway
Yes. Article Tenth names "towns, cities and boroughs" together four times and calls all three "such political subdivisions"; the Constitution gives boroughs the same home-rule standing as towns and cities.

### Cited Findings
- Art. Tenth, "Of Home Rule", § 1 (complete): "The general assembly shall by general law delegate such legislative authority as from time to time it deems appropriate to towns, cities and boroughs relative to the powers, organization, and form of government of such political subdivisions. The general assembly shall from time to time by general law determine the maximum terms of office of the various town, city and borough elective offices. After July 1, 1969, the general assembly shall enact no special legislation relative to the powers, organization, terms of elective offices or form of government of any single town, city or borough, except as to (a) borrowing power, (b) validating acts, and (c) formation, consolidation or dissolution of any town, city or borough, unless in the delegation of legislative authority by general law the general assembly shall have failed to prescribe the powers necessary to effect the purpose of such special legislation." — [Constitution of the State of Connecticut, SOTS Register and Manual](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-i/constitution-of-the-state-of-connecticut)
- Art. Tenth § 2 (complete): "The general assembly may prescribe the methods by which towns, cities and boroughs may establish regional governments and the methods by which towns, cities, boroughs and regional governments may enter into compacts. The general assembly shall prescribe the powers, organization, form, and method of dissolution of any government so established." — [same](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-i/constitution-of-the-state-of-connecticut)
- Art. Eleventh § 2 also lists boroughs among local governments: "Neither the general assembly nor any county, city, borough, town or school district shall have power to pay or grant any extra compensation to any public officer ..." (the page notes § 2 was amended in 1982 by Art. XIX of the Amendments). — [same](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-i/constitution-of-the-state-of-connecticut)

### Inferences
- Special taxing districts, fire districts and associations are not named in Article Tenth. Constitutional home-rule status is therefore a real legal line between boroughs (and the City of Groton) on one side and special districts such as the Groton Long Point Association on the other.

### Gaps
- The cga.ct.gov copy of the Constitution could not be located (guessed URLs returned 404); the Secretary of the State's Register and Manual copy was used instead. An earlier search summary gave "shall by law delegate" for the first sentence; the Secretary of the State text reads "shall by general law delegate".

## 4. Do borough residents vote in town elections and pay town taxes as well as borough taxes?

### Takeaway
Yes on both, by the structure of the statutes: an "elector" is by definition admitted "in a town", town registrars prepare the lists used in boroughs, the borough's tax is laid on the town's grand list and is "in addition to the host town's tax", and state law caps the "combined" borough-plus-town motor vehicle mill rate. No single section says "borough residents are town residents" in so many words; the nearest plain statements are OLR's.

- C.G.S. § 9-1(e): "'Elector' means any person possessing the qualifications prescribed by the Constitution and duly admitted to, and entitled to exercise, the privileges of an elector in a town". — [C.G.S. § 9-1](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_141.htm#sec_9-1)
- C.G.S. § 9-54: "In those towns having cities or boroughs within, and not coterminous with, their limits, the registrars of voters shall also prepare such lists for use in such cities or boroughs". — [C.G.S. ch. 143, § 9-54](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_143.htm#sec_9-54)

The total taxes levied by the town and all cities or boroughs located within such town shall be added together, and the sum shall be the denominator of the fraction." — [C.G.S. § 12-80a](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_203.htm#sec_12-80a)
- C.G.S. §§ 12-136, 12-147, 12-165, 12-174 repeatedly distinguish "the selectmen of each town not consolidated with a city or borough" from "the warden and burgesses of each borough", each acting for its own tax. — [C.G.S. ch. 204](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_204.htm)
- OLR Report 95-R-0800 (8 August 1995): "The General Assembly responded by creating boroughs and cities within the urban areas of the town to provide the services that the residents desired. The boroughs and cities would levy an a tax [sic], in addition to the host town's tax, on their residents to pay for the new or increased services." — [OLR 95-R-0800](https://cga.ct.gov/PS95/rpt/olr/htm/95-R-0800.htm)
- OLR Report 2023-R-0245 (19 October 2023), on special taxing districts and special services districts: "The property taxes both levy are in addition to those levied by their respective municipalities." — [OLR 2023-R-0245](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2023/rpt/pdf/2023-R-0245.pdf)

### Inferences
- Dual voting and dual taxation are confirmed for Connecticut boroughs from primary text. A borough resident is an elector "in a town", votes in town elections, and separately votes in a borough election (usually the first Monday in May of odd years, per the Register and Manual); the borough tax is a second levy on the same town grand list.
- This matches the New York description ("its residents continue to be residents and taxpayers of that town") and the earlier note's conclusion. It also matches how Connecticut special taxing districts work, so dual taxation alone does not separate a borough from a district.

### Gaps
- No Secretary of the State or OPM page was found that states in one sentence that borough residents vote in town elections and pay town taxes. The conclusion rests on the statutory structure above and on OLR's "in addition to the host town's tax".
- Borough charters were not read to confirm who may vote at borough meetings (OLR says "Voters, and in some boroughs property owners").

## 5. How OLR describes boroughs, the City of Groton and special taxing districts; Groton Long Point; Woodmont

### Takeaway
OLR treats boroughs, the unconsolidated City of Groton and special-act districts as points on one continuum ("differences in degree not of kind"), all created by special act inside a host town and all taxing in addition to the town. Groton Long Point is not a borough in Connecticut law: it is the Groton Long Point Association, created by Special Law 280 of 1921, which OLR analyses as a special tax district. Woodmont is a borough inside the consolidated City (and town) of Milford.

### Cited Findings
- OLR Report 95-R-0800, section "Associations, Tax Districts, and Cities Within Towns": "The distinctions between cities within towns (like Groton) and special tax districts (or associations) are differences in degree not of kind. The distinction is best understood by observing the functions of the two entities. Generally, special districts provide one or two functions or services while unconsolidated cities provide an array of services, but both exist within a host municipality and historically both were created by a special act which delineated their boundaries and powers." ## 7. Vermont: village residents are town residents who vote in town meeting and pay town taxes

### Takeaway
Vermont statute says it directly. Village voters are defined as town-meeting voters who live in the village (24 V.S.A. § 1303), the village checklist is copied automatically from the town checklist (17 V.S.A. § 2126), a village is created by vote in town meeting (§ 1301), and village taxes are laid on the town's grand list (for example the Bellows Falls charter, 24 App. V.S.A. ch. 207 § 15). This confirms the VLCT paraphrase the earlier note relied on.

The residents in such village shall thereupon become a body politic and corporate with the powers incident to a public corporation, be known by the name given in such description, by that name may sue and be sued, and hold and convey real and personal estate for the use of the corporation." — [24 V.S.A. § 1301](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/24/039/01301)

- Section 1303 makes village suffrage derivative of town suffrage: one cannot be a village voter without being a town-meeting voter. Together with § 2126 this is primary-text confirmation of VLCT's "legal voters of both the village and the town".
- The statutes do not contain a sentence saying "village property is subject to town taxes". It follows from the structure: the village has no grand list of its own, its tax is an added levy on the part of the town grand list inside its bounds (§ 1309; Bellows Falls § 15), and nothing in ch. 39 exempts village property from the town levy. VLCT's paraphrase on this point is consistent with the text but remains the only one-sentence statement of it.
- Vermont villages differ from Connecticut boroughs in one structural respect: Vermont has a general incorporation statute (§ 1301, by petition in town meeting) as well as special-act charters, and a merger needs a majority in the village and in the town separately (§ 1485(b)); Connecticut boroughs exist only by special act, and consolidation needs only a town-wide majority (C.G.S. § 7-199).
- The Vermont merger chapter's own definition groups incorporated villages with "special purpose districts, and school districts" as "municipalities within a town", much as Connecticut's § 7-195 groups boroughs with districts as "political subdivisions" of a town.

- No Vermont statute was found (in the sections read) that states expressly that village property pays town taxes; Title 32 grand-list provisions were not searched.
- Only one village charter (Bellows Falls) was read. 17 V.S.A. § 2103 was read through an extraction that captured the definitions quoted; its subdivision numbers were not all verified.

## 8. Is a Connecticut borough the same kind of entity as a New York village, or something lesser?

### Takeaway
On the primary text, a Connecticut borough is the same legal kind as a New York village: a chartered general-purpose municipal corporation inside a town, named in the state constitution's home-rule article, holding the same enumerated powers as towns and cities, whose residents remain town electors and town taxpayers. It is lesser only in degree and in autonomy over its own existence: it exists by special act, the town assesses for it, statute calls it a "political subdivision" of the town, and consolidation is decided by the whole town.

### Cited Findings
- Same powers as towns and cities: C.G.S. § 7-148(a) and (c) (quoted in section 1). — [C.G.S. § 7-148](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_098.htm#sec_7-148)
- Constitutional standing: Art. Tenth § 1, "towns, cities and boroughs ... such political subdivisions" (quoted in section 3). — [Constitution, SOTS](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-i/constitution-of-the-state-of-connecticut)
- Subordinate placement: C.G.S. § 7-195(a), "'unit of local government' means a town or political subdivision thereof and 'political subdivision' means a city, borough or district within a town." — [C.G.S. § 7-195](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_099.htm#sec_7-195)
- Continuum with districts: OLR 95-R-0800, "differences in degree not of kind" (quoted in section 5). — [OLR 95-R-0800](https://cga.ct.gov/PS95/rpt/olr/htm/95-R-0800.htm)
- Breadth of function: OLR 98-R-0660, boroughs "provide a range of services, except education. They construct and maintain streets, provide police and fire protection, and health and sanitation services." — [OLR 98-R-0660](https://www.cga.ct.gov/PS98/rpt/olr/htm/98-r-0660.htm)

### Inferences
- The earlier note's conclusion ("In legal kind the four are the same") is confirmed for Connecticut by primary text. Nothing read contradicts it.
- The earlier note's inference that the borough is "closer to a special taxing or service district with general-purpose form" is supported as a description of practice (and by OLR's continuum language) but not as a statement of legal status: in law a borough has general municipal powers and constitutional home-rule standing that a special taxing district lacks.
- A defensible classification from the Connecticut sources is three tiers inside a town: (a) borough or unconsolidated city, a municipality under § 7-148 and Art. Tenth (the eight separate boroughs and the City of Groton); (b) special-act association or district with broad powers (Groton Long Point Association); (c) statutory special taxing districts under §§ 7-324 to 7-329. Groton Long Point belongs to (b), so a rule that treats boroughs like New York villages would not by itself reach Groton Long Point, while a rule keyed to the Census "borough" label would.

### Gaps
- New York Village Law text was not re-read in this round; the comparison uses the New York Department of State description quoted in the earlier note.
- No Connecticut court decision on the legal nature of a borough was read (CourtListener was not searched).
