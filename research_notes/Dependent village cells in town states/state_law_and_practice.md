# State law and practice: Vermont villages and Connecticut boroughs versus New York and Michigan villages

Research date: 17 November 2026. Verbatim quotes marked (PDF) were read directly from the extracted PDF text. Quotes marked (fetch) came back through a page-summarising fetch tool and are short excerpts; quotes marked (snippet) come from search-result summaries only and should be re-checked before being quoted in a final report. Population figures marked (project file) are 2020 Census counts (`pop20`) tabulated from this repository's `data/places-sf.parquet`, which is built from Census TIGER place records.

## 1. Vermont: what an incorporated village is, what it does, how many remain

### Takeaway
A Vermont incorporated village is a full municipal corporation in state law ("municipality" in 1 V.S.A. § 126) sitting inside a town; its residents vote and pay taxes in both, and it typically supplies denser-area services (water, sewer, electric, sometimes police and fire). About 28 to 30 remain, down from roughly 76 ever created, all are small (largest 2,747; median about 660), and the trend is steady merger into the parent town.

### Cited Findings

- Title 24, chapter 39 is titled "Incorporated Villages" and runs §§ 1301 to 1321, including § 1301 "Establishment of villages", § 1303 "Voters", § 1308 "Duties of trustees; vacancies", § 1309 "Taxes; assessment and collection", § 1310 "Bylaws", § 1315 "Appointment of manager", and §§ 1316 to 1321 on annexation. Section text was not retrieved (only titles). — [Vermont Statutes Online, 24 V.S.A. ch. 39](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/24/039)
- VLCT (fetch): "only incorporated villages are considered municipalities under 1 V.S.A. 126"; incorporated villages are "a unique form of local government"; unincorporated historic villages are "not municipalities". — [VLCT](https://www.vlct.org/node/4222)
- VLCT (fetch): "villages can't exist separately from the towns of which they are a part." — [VLCT](https://www.vlct.org/node/4222)

### Inferences
- The 28 (VLCT) versus 29 active (Census) versus 30 (Wikipedia, 2024) discrepancy is probably explained by timing and by Jericho (flagged inactive by Census) and Westbury (in VLCT/Wikipedia, absent from Census). The safe statement is "about 28 to 30 as of 2024 to 2026".
- About 76 to 80 villages have existed (roughly 30 current plus about 50 former in the Wikipedia tables), so well over half have been absorbed or converted; the rate since 1984 is about one merger every two years. The stock is small and shrinking, but not vanishing quickly.
- In law a Vermont village is not a lesser thing than a New York or Michigan village: it is a chartered municipal corporation with its own voters, trustees, taxes and bylaws, layered on a town whose government continues to operate inside it.
- Vermont villages are a small share of state population: 24,581 of roughly 643,000 (2020), about 4 percent (state total recalled from the 2020 Census, not re-fetched).

### Gaps
- Full text of 24 V.S.A. §§ 1303, 1309 and 1314 was not retrieved; the "voters of both" and "town grand list" statements rest on VLCT's paraphrase.
- The Secretary of State / State Archives village page could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so the State's own current count and list were not read directly.
- VCGI: its "VT Geographic Area Names and Codes Standard" PDF surfaced in search but was not read; how VCGI's boundary layers treat villages (separate village layer versus town layer) is unverified.
- Westbury: the creation of a new village in Colchester in 2023/2024 is reported by VLCT and Wikipedia but was not verified against a statute or act, and Census does not list it.
- Whether Poultney or Jericho villages have completed mergers by late 2026 was not established. Census flags Jericho village as inactive, which suggests it no longer functions, but VLCT says the merger is only under consideration.
- Service-by-service inventory (which villages run water, sewer, electric, fire, zoning) was not compiled. Several villages are known for village electric departments, but no source was read for a list.

## 2. Connecticut: what a borough is, what it does, how many remain

### Takeaway
A Connecticut borough is an incorporated municipal corporation inside a town, with a warden and burgesses, its own tax levy and usually a narrow set of functions (streets, fire, zoning, parks); police and schools are almost always the town's. Nine remain on the Secretary of the State's list (one, Naugatuck, consolidated with its town), plus the City of Groton as the only non-consolidated city; the eight separate boroughs hold only about 13,800 people in total.

### Cited Findings
- Secretary of the State, Register and Manual, "Boroughs in Connecticut with Date of Incorporation" (fetch): Stonington (May 1801), Newtown (May 1824), Danielson (Killingly) (May 1854), Litchfield (Jan. 1879), Naugatuck (Jan. 1893), Jewett City (Griswold) (Jan. 1895), Fenwick (Old Saybrook) (Jan. 1899), Woodmont (Milford) (Jan. 1903), Bantam (Litchfield) (Jan. 1915). Note on the table: "Where the name of the borough is other than the town in which it is located, the town location is given in parentheses." The same page lists 21 cities, including Groton ("Home Rule Act, May 4, 1964"). The page states no count of towns. — [CT Secretary of the State, Register and Manual, Section VII](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections)
- Census of Governments, 2022 Individual State Descriptions, Connecticut (PDF): "The subcounty general purpose governments in Connecticut consist of municipal (city and borough) governments and town governments. These two types of governments are distinguished primarily by the historical circumstances surrounding their incorporation. In Connecticut, city, borough, and town governments have similar powers and perform similar functions." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Same source (PDF): "The Constitution of the State of Connecticut authorizes cities, boroughs, towns, consolidated towns and cities, or consolidated towns and boroughs to adopt home rule charters." A legislative body may be, among others, "a board of burgesses" and a chief executive may be "an elected warden". — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Same source (PDF): "Cities are located within town areas. The city of Groton is located within a town area but exists separately and is counted as a separate municipal government. For the other 19 cities, the city and town government have been consolidated." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Same source (PDF): "Boroughs are also located within town areas. Most boroughs exist separately from the overlying town government and are counted as separate municipal governments. The borough of Naugatuck is consolidated with the overlying town government and is counted only once as a borough government. The borough of Woodmont is located within the overlying city (city-town consolidation) of Milford, exists separately, and also is counted only as a borough government." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Same source (PDF): "The entire area of the state is encompassed by town areas... Towns in Connecticut provide the services that are associated with counties and municipalities in most states." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Note the ISD says "the other 19 cities" (20 cities in total) while the Secretary of the State table lists 21 cities; the difference was not resolved. — [Census ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf); [SOTS](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections)
- 2020 populations (project file): Naugatuck borough 31,539 (consolidated, coextensive with the town); Groton city 9,387; Danielson 4,155; Jewett City 3,328; Newtown borough 1,914; Woodmont 1,486; Litchfield borough 1,179; Stonington borough 976; Bantam 720; Fenwick 53. Census also carries a tenth active borough record, "Groton Long Point borough" (530), which is not on the Secretary of the State's borough list. — [Census TIGER/Line place files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/tiger-line-file.html)
- History (fetch), Edward T. Howe (Professor of Economics, Emeritus, Siena): a borough was "an incorporated borough that would be located within and subordinate to the town government", created to provide "public services that its parent town would not or could not afford"; "Altogether, between 1800 and 1921, 45 boroughs were created"; "15 of them disincorporated from their parent town, 15 eventually became cities"; "Of the nine boroughs that currently exist", six (Naugatuck, Bantam, Litchfield, Fenwick, Woodmont, Stonington) "appear to be working fairly well" while Newtown, Danielson and Jewett City "have encountered budgetary pressures in previous years". — [New England Historical Society, "The Rise and Fall of the Connecticut Borough"](https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/rise-fall-connecticut-borough/)
- Recent disincorporations listed by Wikipedia: Colchester 1990, Stafford Springs 1991, Willimantic (the article gives 1983 in the text and 1893 in the table, an internal inconsistency), Wallingford 1958, Southington 1948, Farmington and Unionville 1947, Guilford 1941, New Canaan 1935, Greenwich 1932. — [Wikipedia, Borough (Connecticut)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_(Connecticut))
- Wikipedia's characterisation (fetch): "Borough governments are not autonomous and are subordinate to the government of the town to which they belong"; a borough is "a clearly defined municipality and provides some municipal services, such as police and fire services"; "Of the current boroughs, one (Naugatuck) is consolidated with its town." — [Wikipedia, Borough (Connecticut)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_(Connecticut))
- Stonington Borough (snippets from borough documents): it "lies completely within the physical boundaries of the Town of Stonington yet has its own governing body"; "Borough residents are also residents of the Town of Stonington and are served by the Town police department, educational system, registrar of vital statistics, land records, and more"; "The Town does not maintain Borough streets but instead returns a portion of our town taxes to the Borough"; the Stonington Borough Fire Department "is funded through the Borough budget"; the budget is approved by voters at an annual borough meeting in April. — [Borough of Stonington](https://www.stoningtonboroughct.gov/copy-of-governance) and borough guide PDFs on the same site (via search summary)
- Stonington Borough governance page (fetch): "The governing body of the Borough consists of five elected officials: the Warden and four Burgesses." Commissioner portfolios listed: Public Safety ("Includes Police & Fire"), Streets & Sidewalks, Parks, Trees & Rights of Way, Public Buildings, Public Utilities & Sanitation; the borough has its own Planning & Zoning Commission and Zoning Board of Appeals, and a Clerk-Treasurer / Assessor. — [Borough of Stonington](https://www.stoningtonboroughct.gov/copy-of-governance)
- Fenwick (snippets from the borough's site): governed by "a Board of Warden and six Burgesses elected in May of every odd numbered year"; "Fenwick receives police, school, and volunteer fire and ambulance services from Old Saybrook." — [Borough of Fenwick](https://www.boroughoffenwick.com) (via search summary; the /about page returned 404 on direct fetch)
- Elections: the Secretary of the State publishes borough and city tables inside the "Town Elections" section of the Register and Manual, and the State's election-history site carries separate borough contests. — [CT SOTS Register and Manual](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections); [CT Election History](https://electionhistory.ct.gov/contest/37999)
- State aid: OPM publishes municipal grant history "by Town" and "Estimates of State Formula Aid to Municipalities". — [CT OPM, Grant History by Town](https://portal.ct.gov/OPM/IGPP/Grants/Grant-History-by-Town/Grant-History-by-Town); [CT OPM, Estimates of State Formula Aid](https://portal.ct.gov/opm/igpp/grants/grants)

### Inferences
- The eight non-consolidated boroughs on the State's list total 13,811 people (2020), about 0.4 percent of Connecticut's population; adding the City of Groton and Groton Long Point gives about 23,700. Whatever rule is chosen for Connecticut affects a very small number of people and events.
- Functionally the Connecticut borough is the thinnest of the four entity types: Stonington and Fenwick both say police and schools come from the town, and the borough's own functions are streets, fire, zoning and amenities. This is closer to a special taxing or service district with general-purpose form than to a New York village with a police department and a court.
- The State's own organising unit is the town: grant and fiscal tables are "by Town", and the Register lists boroughs as an appendix table to town elections. Boroughs are nonetheless legally municipalities with home-rule charter capacity.
- Naugatuck is a non-issue for the cell question because borough and town are coextensive and consolidated.

### Gaps
- Connecticut General Statutes text was not retrieved (the cga.ct.gov chapter 98 page failed on a certificate error). The commonly cited definition in C.G.S. § 7-148(a) ("municipality" includes any town, city or borough, consolidated town and city or consolidated town and borough) is from memory and should be verified.
- No State source was read that states "169 towns" in so many words; the Register page fetched does not give a count. That figure is standard but uncited here.
- Whether OPM formula-aid tables list boroughs as separate grantees (some programs historically list boroughs and fire districts as separate lines below the 169 towns) was not verified; the OPM tables were not opened.
- No sources were read for what Litchfield, Bantam, Newtown, Jewett City, Danielson and Woodmont boroughs each do. Howe notes budget pressure in Newtown, Danielson and Jewett City but gives no functional detail.
- Groton Long Point: why Census carries it as a borough while the Secretary of the State does not list it (it is usually described as an association with municipal powers created by special act) was not researched.

## 3. New York: village law basics, scale, services, "town outside village"

### Takeaway
New York villages are general-purpose municipal corporations inside towns; residents remain town residents, voters and taxpayers. There are about 530 (532 in current Census records; 535 in 2018 per the State handbook), with 1.9 million residents, and state law and finance are built around the distinction between town-wide and "town outside village" (TOV) functions.

### Cited Findings
- NY Department of State, Local Government Handbook, 2024 reprint (PDF): "In New York State, the village is a general purpose municipal corporation formed voluntarily by the residents of an area in one or more towns to provide themselves with municipal services. But when a village is created, its area still remains a part of the town where it is located, and its residents continue to be residents and taxpayers of that town." — [NY DOS Local Government Handbook (2024)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "In New York State, a village is a legal concept; it is a municipal corporation. The largest village in the state, Hempstead in Nassau County, had more than 53,000 residents in 2010, while the smallest city, Sherrill, had 3,071." — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "There were 535 villages in New York State in 2018. They range in size from the Village of Dering Harbor with a 2010 Census population of 11, to the Village of Hempstead, with a 2010 Census population of 53,891. The majority of villages have populations under 2,500, although there were 24 villages between 10,000 and 20,000 population in 2010, and 11 villages with more than 20,000 population." Also: "As of December 31, 2017 the number of villages were 536". — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "There are 72 villages located in two towns and 5 villages are located in three towns. There are nine villages which are in two counties." — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF), on dissolution: "27 new villages appeared and 26 villages dissolved between 1940 and December 31, 2012." — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF), section 7.4.2 "Part-town Organization": "Services for part of the town may be rendered to either all of that portion of the town outside villages (TOV), or to particular areas of the town by way of improvement districts or improvement areas. Until recent decades, the only major service that towns were required to provide to town residents living outside villages was highway maintenance." The handbook's population table has separate rows for "Villages" and "Towns Outside of Villages". — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Same (PDF): the mayor is responsible for "enforcing laws within the village and for supervising the police and other officers of the village". — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- Office of the State Comptroller (snippet): "Villages, in contrast, are located within towns, and their residents pay taxes to both the village and town." — [NY OSC, municipal structures report](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/local-government/documents/pdf/2019-02/munistructures.pdf)
- Census ISD, New York (PDF): "The subcounty general purpose governments consist of municipal (city and village) governments and town governments. These two types of governments are distinguished primarily by the historical circumstances surrounding their incorporation. City, town, and village governments have similar powers and perform similar functions." And: "Unlike cities, which exist outside the area of any town, villages are included within town areas and are subject to town taxes for general government functions. City and village governments have broad home-rule powers." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Census ISD footnote (PDF): where a town is coterminous with a village (East Rochester, Scarsdale, Mount Kisco) "the village officers also serve as town officers" and the unit is counted once. — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Scale (project file, 2020 Census): 532 village records; total population 1,925,950; median 1,743; range 50 to 59,169; 176 under 1,000; 323 under 2,500; 109 at 5,000 or more; 40 at 10,000 or more. — [Census TIGER/Line place files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/tiger-line-file.html)

### Inferences
- New York is the one state of the four where the village/TOV split is embedded in state administrative practice (budget funds, sales-tax allocation, zoning and planning jurisdiction, published population tables). That is a State-endorsed precedent for exactly the "village cell plus town-remainder cell" partition.
- New York villages are an order of magnitude larger in aggregate (about 9.5 percent of state population, using a 2020 state total of about 20.2 million recalled from memory) and include dozens of places above 10,000; nothing comparable exists in Vermont or Connecticut after Essex Junction's departure (Naugatuck aside).

### Gaps
- Village Law section-level citations (incorporation thresholds, § 8-800 police departments, Article 17-A GML dissolution) were not read directly; § 8-800 and GML § 121-a are from search summaries.
- A current (2025 or 2026) official village count was not found; 535 (2018, handbook) and 532 (Census records) bracket it.

## 4. Michigan: villages, general law versus home rule, the township's continuing role

### Takeaway
Michigan has about 252 villages (206 general law, 46 home rule). They stay inside the township; the township still assesses property, collects county and school taxes and runs elections for village residents, while the village does local ordinances, police, fire, public works and utilities. Their scale (median about 700, total about 271,000) is almost identical to Vermont's villages.

### Cited Findings
- Michigan Municipal League, Handbook for Municipal Officials 2024, ch. 2 (PDF): "The basic difference between a city and a village is that wherever an area is incorporated as a village, it stays within the township. Village residents participate in township affairs and pay township taxes in addition to having their own village government. Incorporation as a city, however, removes an area from township government." — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "Villages in Michigan are organized primarily to establish local regulatory ordinances and to provide local services such as fire and police protection, public works, and utilities. Certain local duties required by the state are performed by the embracing township, including assessing property; collecting taxes for counties and school districts; and administering village, county, state, and national elections. Most villages (206 of 252) are still governed under the General Law Village Act." — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "As of 2024, Michigan had 281 incorporated cities and 252 incorporated villages—a total of 533 municipalities." "Of the 252 villages in Michigan, 46 have home rule charters, and 206 are governed under the General Law Village Act (1895 PA 3)." "Of the 46 home rule villages, only 18 have a village manager position." — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf)
- Same (PDF): "The trend has also developed in villages to seek incorporation as cities whereby they achieve a separation of jurisdiction from the township." — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf)
- Michigan Manual 2021-2022 (PDF): "Villages are another type of local government. They have many of the special powers of cities but not the duties the state demands, and so villages remain part of the townships in which they are located. Village residents participate in township affairs and pay township taxes." And: "Villages differ from cities in that villages are not legally separated from the township and are not required to assess property for tax purposes or conduct state and national elections." — [Michigan Manual 2021-2022, ch. VIII](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2021-2022/michiganmanual/2021-MM-P0657-P0659.pdf)
- Michigan Manual (PDF): "At the local level, cities, villages, and townships exercise the primary governing authority. The three units have similar, but not identical, service and regulatory powers." — [Michigan Manual 2021-2022](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2021-2022/michiganmanual/2021-MM-P0657-P0659.pdf)
- Census ISD, Michigan (PDF): "Villages operate under either home rule charters or general law. Villages are included within township governments; that is, villagers participate in township affairs and pay township taxes in addition to having their own village government. Villages are organized primarily to establish local regulatory ordinances and to provide utilities and other local services such as fire and police protection and public works." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Scale (project file, 2020 Census): 253 village records; total population 270,795; median 712; range 104 to 10,584; 163 under 1,000; 234 under 2,500; only 5 at 5,000 or more. — [Census TIGER/Line place files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/tiger-line-file.html)
- General Law Village Act authorises the village council to establish a police force, with village officers having sheriff-like powers within the village (search summary of MCL 70.13 and 70.16). — [MCL 70.16](https://zeus-milenia.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/MCL-70-16.pdf)

### Inferences
- Michigan villages are, if anything, more dependent on the surrounding township than Vermont villages are on their town: the township does their assessing and runs their elections. Yet Michigan sources uniformly list villages alongside cities as "municipalities" (281 cities + 252 villages = 533).
- Michigan and Vermont villages are the same size class: medians 712 and 661; share of state population about 2.7 percent (Michigan, using roughly 10.08 million in 2020, recalled) versus about 3.8 percent (Vermont). An argument that Vermont villages are "too small to matter" would apply equally to Michigan.

### Gaps
- No Michigan Townships Association publication was read.
- A 2026 village count was not found; MML gives 252 as of 2024 and Census records give 253.

## 5. How the states and Census count and list their municipalities

### Takeaway
All four states treat villages/boroughs as legal municipalities, but the two New England states organise their lists around the town (Vermont: 247 cities and towns, with villages counted among "other municipal entities"; Connecticut: town-keyed registers and grant tables with boroughs as an appended table), whereas New York and Michigan list villages as a primary tier (Michigan: "533 municipalities" = cities + villages; New York: separate "Villages" and "Towns Outside of Villages" rows).

### Cited Findings
- VLCT 2025 summary (snippet): "all 247 Vermont cities and towns are members of VLCT, as are 140 other municipal entities that include villages, solid waste districts, and fire districts." — [VLCT 2025 summary](https://www.vlct.org/sites/default/files/uploads/vlct_summary_2025.docx)
- VLCT lists "Incorporated Villages = 28" as its own line. — [VLCT](https://www.vlct.org/node/4222)
- A Vermont town website describes "the 251 towns and cities in the State of Vermont" (snippet), a figure that differs from VLCT's 247 because it includes unorganised towns/gores in the traditional "251 Club" count. — [East Montpelier, Local Law Enforcement](https://eastmontpeliervt.org/community/local-law-enforcement/)
- Census ISD, Vermont (PDF): "Subcounty general purpose governments in Vermont include municipal (city and village) governments and town governments... The term 'municipality' as defined for Census Bureau statistics on governments applies to the cities and incorporated villages in Vermont. Incorporated villages are governed by a board of trustees and cities are governed by a mayor and board of aldermen. Cities exist outside the area of any town. Villages are included in town areas. Towns, called 'municipalities' by Vermont statutes, are counted in Census Bureau statistics as township rather than municipal governments." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Census ISD, Connecticut (PDF): "The term 'municipality' applies only to the boroughs and cities in Connecticut as defined in Census Bureau statistics... Towns, which are treated as municipalities in Connecticut statutes, are counted as separate governments." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Connecticut Register and Manual presents towns as the main list and cities and boroughs as dated tables within the "Town Elections" section. — [CT SOTS](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections)
- Connecticut OPM presents municipal grants as "Grant History by Town". — [CT OPM](https://portal.ct.gov/OPM/IGPP/Grants/Grant-History-by-Town/Grant-History-by-Town)
- Michigan: "281 incorporated cities and 252 incorporated villages—a total of 533 municipalities" (MML, 2024); the Michigan Manual describes cities, villages and townships as the three units exercising "the primary governing authority". — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf); [Michigan Manual](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2021-2022/michiganmanual/2021-MM-P0657-P0659.pdf)
- New York: the DOS handbook counts villages (535 in 2018) as one of the four general-purpose types (counties, cities, towns, villages) and tabulates population separately for "Villages" and "Towns Outside of Villages". — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- The listing conventions do differ in the direction the project's current rule assumes: in Vermont and Connecticut the canonical exhaustive list (247 cities and towns; the town list) already partitions the state with no village layer needed, and villages are an overlay. In New York and Michigan official tabulations routinely split the town into village and non-village parts.
- This is a difference of convention and emphasis, not of legal kind: none of the sources says a Vermont village or Connecticut borough is not a municipality.

### Gaps
- The Vermont Secretary of State's own headline count (for example "237 towns, 10 cities, N villages") was not read; with Essex Junction's 2022 charter Vermont has 10 cities, and VLCT's "247 cities and towns" implies 237 towns, but the State's own wording is unverified.
- VCGI and Connecticut state GIS conventions (whether the official boundary layer is towns only) were not checked.
- The 2022 Census of Governments numeric counts of municipal governments by state (which would show how many villages/boroughs Census counted as active governments) were not extracted.

## 6. Policing: which villages and boroughs have their own police

### Takeaway
Own-force policing is common in New York villages (at least 100, plausibly a few hundred, of about 530), authorised and widespread but unquantified here for Michigan villages, rare in Vermont (two or three of about 29 villages) and essentially absent in Connecticut's separate boroughs (police come from the town or State Police), with the City of Groton and Groton Long Point as the exceptions.

### Cited Findings
- Vermont has 53 municipal police departments (VLCT, June 2024). Departments named in the VLCT survey table include "Bellows Falls PD", "Swanton Village PD", "Woodstock PD", "Lyndonville PD", "Moristown PD" [sic], "Manchester PD", "Northfield PD", "Essex PD" and, separately, "Town of Rockingham PD". Three counties (Grand Isle, Essex and one other) have no municipal police departments. — [VLCT, A Snapshot of Municipal Policing in Vermont, June 2024](https://www.vlct.org/sites/default/files/uploads/a-snapshot-of-municipal-policing-in-vermont-june-2024.pdf)
- Wikipedia's Vermont list has 54 municipal entries; the only one explicitly tagged as a village department is "Swanton Village Police Department (Village of Swanton)"; it also lists Bellows Falls, Woodstock, Ludlow, Lyndonville, Manchester (tagged Town of Manchester), Morristown, Enosburgh and Northfield departments. — [Wikipedia, List of law enforcement agencies in Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_law_enforcement_agencies_in_Vermont)
- The Bellows Falls village charter authorises a village police department under a chief of police (search summary). — [24 App. V.S.A. ch. 207 § 17](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/24APPENDIX/207/00017)
- Most Vermont towns have no police: "approximately 200 towns" of 251 are without their own force and rely on the Vermont State Police by default (snippet). — [East Montpelier, Local Law Enforcement](https://eastmontpeliervt.org/community/local-law-enforcement/)
- Connecticut: Wikipedia's list (flagged incomplete) includes City of Groton Police Department, Town of Groton Police Department, Groton Long Point Police Department, Naugatuck Police Department, Stonington Police Department (town) and Newtown Police Department (town); it lists no Woodmont, Fenwick, Jewett City, Danielson, Litchfield or Bantam departments. — [Wikipedia, List of law enforcement agencies in Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_law_enforcement_agencies_in_Connecticut)
- Stonington Borough residents "are served by the Town police department" (snippet); the Stonington Police Department is a town agency overseen by a town Board of Police Commissioners, headquartered in Pawcatuck. — [Borough of Stonington](https://www.stoningtonboroughct.gov/copy-of-governance); [Town of Stonington Police](https://www.stonington-ct.gov/stonington-police)
- "Fenwick receives police, school, and volunteer fire and ambulance services from Old Saybrook" (snippet). — [Borough of Fenwick](https://www.boroughoffenwick.com)
- New York: "Over 400 separate county, city, town and village police agencies share responsibility for the enforcement of state and local laws in New York", including "11 agencies with only one or two part-time police officers". — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf)
- New York: Wikipedia's municipal list has about 400 departments outside New York City; in the first roughly 70 percent of the page about 100 are explicitly tagged "(Village of ...)", about 40 as towns and about 25 as cities, with many untagged, so about 100 is a lower bound for village departments. Examples: Adams, Addison, Afton, Albion, Alfred, Andover, Athens, Attica, Avon, Bath, Boonville, Catskill. — [Wikipedia, List of law enforcement agencies in New York (state)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_law_enforcement_agencies_in_New_York_(state))
- New York law gives village police fiscal significance: Village Law § 8-800 authorises village police departments; General Municipal Law § 121-a authorises joint town-village departments; under Town Law § 150 a town department created after 1 January 1960 may not charge village property where the village maintains its own department of four or more officers (search summaries of a 1995 Attorney General opinion). — [NY AG Informal Opinion 95-43](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/opinions/I_95-43_pw.pdf); [GML § 121-a](https://law.justia.com/codes/new-york/2019/gmu/article-6/121-a/index.html)
- New York DCJS-derived agency directories list individual "Village Police Department" agencies (Geneseo, Adams, Greenwich, Brockport, Lloyd Harbor, Arcade, Endicott, Amityville, Le Roy, Hamilton, Black River, Penn Yan, Perry and others), confirming that village departments are separately registered reporting agencies. — [opendatany.com criminal justice directory](https://opendatany.com/criminal-justice.php)
- Michigan: villages are "organized primarily to establish local regulatory ordinances and to provide local services such as fire and police protection, public works, and utilities" (MML). A 2024 Michigan Public Policy Survey report found about 27 percent of all Michigan cities, townships and villages directly provide law enforcement (snippet; not village-specific). — [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf); [CLOSUP MPPS Spring 2024](https://closup.umich.edu/sites/closup/files/2025-01/MPPS-Spring-2024-Funding-Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rough shares of dependent villages/boroughs with their own police: Vermont about 2 to 3 of 29 (Swanton Village and Bellows Falls clearly; Woodstock probably a village department; Morristown, Manchester, Ludlow and Northfield appear to be town departments; Lyndonville's was a village department before the 2023 merger), so under about 10 percent. Connecticut 0 of 8 separate boroughs on the State's list (plus City of Groton and Groton Long Point, which do have forces). New York at least about 20 percent and probably 40 to 50 percent (if about 400 agencies less about 57 sheriffs, about 60 cities and roughly 100 towns are villages). Michigan unknown, likely a substantial minority. These are estimates, not sourced counts.
- For arrest-location reporting, an arresting or assisting local agency named after a village (as in New York) is a reason the village name will appear in records; in Vermont and Connecticut that mechanism is nearly absent, since the local agency, where any exists, is usually the town's, and otherwise the State Police.
- The VLCT table listing both "Bellows Falls PD" and "Town of Rockingham PD" suggests even in Vermont the village/town split can show up in agency names; this was not investigated further.

### Gaps
- FBI Crime Data Explorer agency lists were not consulted, so which villages/boroughs appear as ORIs in UCR/NIBRS is unverified for all four states.
- No official count of village police departments was found for New York (DCJS) or Michigan (MCOLES); both agencies publish directories that could be filtered.
- Whether Woodstock PD and Ludlow PD are legally village or town departments was not confirmed. Essex Junction's policing arrangement after separation (it shared services with Essex town until at least 1 July 2023 per Wikipedia's Essex article, via snippet) was not confirmed for 2026.
- Whether Danielson, Jewett City, Litchfield, Bantam and Woodmont have constables, resident state troopers or town police was not sourced. Stonington Borough lists a "Commissioner of Public Safety - Includes Police & Fire", but its residents are served by the town police.

## 7. Same kind or different kind? Authoritative statements

### Takeaway
No source found distinguishes New England villages/boroughs from New York or Michigan villages in kind. The Census Bureau uses near-identical language for Vermont, Connecticut and New York, classing villages and boroughs as "municipal governments" that sit within town areas; the differences that exist are of degree (size, breadth of functions, policing) and of how strong the overlying town is.

### Cited Findings
- Census ISD uses the same formula for three of the four states: municipal governments (city and village, or city and borough) and town governments "are distinguished primarily by the historical circumstances surrounding their incorporation" and "have similar powers and perform similar functions" (Vermont, Connecticut and New York sections, PDF). — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- Parallel structural sentences (PDF): Vermont, "Villages are included in town areas"; Connecticut, "Boroughs are also located within town areas"; New York, "villages are included within town areas and are subject to town taxes for general government functions"; Michigan, "Villages are included within township governments; that is, villagers participate in township affairs and pay township taxes in addition to having their own village government." — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf)
- A difference Census does draw is about the overlying unit, not the village: "Towns in Connecticut provide the services that are associated with counties and municipalities in most states"; Vermont towns "perform many of the same duties commonly associated with county and municipal governments", whereas Michigan's handbook stresses that townships have limited general taxing power and "do not have full territorial integrity or control over the road system". — [Census Bureau, 2022 ISD](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2024/econ/2022ISD.pdf); [Michigan Manual](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2021-2022/michiganmanual/2021-MM-P0657-P0659.pdf)
- Howe treats the Connecticut borough and the Vermont village as the same device and as unusual within New England: Vermont is "the only other New England state that allows similar responsibilities below the town level", and Connecticut itself once had two incorporated villages (Litchfield and Wethersfield) in the nineteenth century. He calls the borough "located within and subordinate to the town government". — [New England Historical Society (E. T. Howe)](https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/rise-fall-connecticut-borough/)
- VLCT calls incorporated villages "a unique form of local government" and stresses "villages can't exist separately from the towns of which they are a part". — [VLCT](https://www.vlct.org/node/4222)
- New York and Michigan handbooks describe the identical dependence: the village "still remains a part of the town" and residents "continue to be residents and taxpayers of that town" (New York); "wherever an area is incorporated as a village, it stays within the township" (Michigan). — [NY DOS Handbook](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/01/localgovernmenthandbook_2024.pdf); [MML Handbook ch. 2](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/HMO-CH-2-Structure-of-Local-Government.pdf)

### Inferences
- In legal kind the four are the same: an incorporated general-purpose municipality nested in, and not removed from, a town or township, with dual voting and dual taxation. A rule that treats Vermont villages and Connecticut boroughs differently from New York and Michigan villages cannot be justified by legal status.
- Defensible grounds for different treatment are differences of degree: (a) the New England town is the strong, primary, universally recognised local government, whereas the Michigan township is the weak residual unit and in New York the State itself splits towns into village and TOV parts; (b) own-force policing is rare to absent in Vermont villages and Connecticut boroughs; (c) the population is tiny (about 24,600 in Vermont; about 13,800 in Connecticut's eight separate boroughs); (d) the long-run direction in both states is merger into the town, with the largest village (Essex Junction) having exited to city status.
- Grounds against different treatment: Vermont villages are the same size class as Michigan villages (medians 661 versus 712) and a larger share of state population; several have distinct, well-known names (Bellows Falls, Morrisville, Enosburg Falls, Orleans); and the States and Census do count them as municipal governments.
- Connecticut is the weaker case for village cells than Vermont: fewer units, narrower functions, town police, and a State administrative culture organised around its towns.

### Gaps
- No law review article or treatise making an explicit cross-state comparison was located.
- The Michigan section of the ISD does not use the "historical circumstances" sentence; whether that omission is meaningful is unknown.

## 8. Everyday identity, naming and addressing

### Takeaway
About half of Vermont villages and four of nine Connecticut boroughs share their town's name, so folding them into the town loses no name; the remainder carry distinct names, several of which (Bellows Falls, Morrisville, Jewett City, Danielson) are the better-known everyday and postal names for the settlement. Systematic evidence on USPS and colloquial usage was not gathered.

### Cited Findings
- Vermont name-sharing, from the Wikipedia table of 31 current villages: 16 share the parent town's name (Woodstock, Ludlow, Barton, Swanton, Johnson, Hyde Park, Newbury, Manchester, Cambridge, Poultney, Newfane, Westminster, Marshfield, Albany, Alburgh, Jericho); 15 differ (Bellows Falls and Saxtons River in Rockingham; North Bennington and Old Bennington in Bennington; North Troy in Troy; Orleans in Barton; Enosburg Falls in Enosburgh; Wells River in Newbury; Morrisville in Morristown; Jeffersonville in Cambridge; Derby Center and Derby Line in Derby; West Burke in Burke; Jacksonville in Whitingham; Westbury in Colchester). — [Wikipedia, Village (Vermont)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Village_(Vermont))
- Connecticut name-sharing, from the Secretary of the State's parenthetical convention: 4 share the town name (Stonington, Newtown, Litchfield, Naugatuck); 5 differ (Danielson in Killingly, Jewett City in Griswold, Fenwick in Old Saybrook, Woodmont in Milford, Bantam in Litchfield). The City of Groton shares the Town of Groton's name. — [CT SOTS Register and Manual](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections)
- Borough Hall for Stonington Borough gives its address as "26 Church Street, Stonington, CT 06378", that is, the postal city name is the shared "Stonington". — [Borough of Stonington](https://stoningtonboroughct.gov/copy-of-borough-officials-1)
- Five Vermont towns contain two villages each or share a parent (Rockingham: Bellows Falls and Saxtons River; Bennington: North Bennington and Old Bennington; Derby: Derby Center and Derby Line; Barton: Barton and Orleans; Newbury: Newbury and Wells River; Cambridge: Cambridge and Jeffersonville); Litchfield, Connecticut contains two boroughs (Litchfield and Bantam). — [Wikipedia, Village (Vermont)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Village_(Vermont)); [CT SOTS](https://portal.ct.gov/sots/register-manual/section-vii/town-elections)

### Inferences
- By 2020 population (project file), the 16 same-name Vermont villages hold 11,625 people and the 14 different-name villages in the Census file hold 12,956. So roughly half of Vermont village residents live in a village whose name differs from the town's; among those, 8 have derivative names (North Bennington, Old Bennington, North Troy, Derby Center, Derby Line, West Burke, Enosburg Falls, Morrisville) and 6 have wholly distinct names (Bellows Falls, Saxtons River, Orleans, Wells River, Jeffersonville, Jacksonville).
- In Connecticut, the different-name boroughs hold 9,742 (Danielson, Jewett City, Woodmont, Bantam, Fenwick) and the same-name separate boroughs 4,069 (Newtown, Litchfield, Stonington).
- For same-name pairs, the fold loses only the "Village of"/"Borough of" qualifier; for different-name pairs it replaces a recognisable settlement name (Bellows Falls, Jewett City, Danielson) with a town name (Rockingham, Griswold, Killingly) that is often less familiar to outsiders. If the project wants a middle course, a secondary label (for example "Rockingham (Bellows Falls)") would preserve the information without changing the cell rule.

### Gaps
- USPS preferred city names were not looked up. It is widely understood that Bellows Falls, Morrisville, Enosburg Falls, Jewett City, Danielson and Bantam are post office names and that Essex Junction is the post office for much of Essex, but no USPS source was fetched.
- No survey, newspaper-usage or dateline evidence was gathered on whether residents identify with the village/borough or the town.
- How ICE or other federal arrest records name locations in these places (village, town or post office name) is outside this note and unverified.
