## 1. For each of the twelve states, which incorporated place types are dependent on a county subdivision and which are independent?

### Takeaway
Seven states have only independent places (ME, MA, NH, RI, NJ, PA, WI). Five have dependent places: CT (all cities and boroughs dependent), VT (villages dependent, cities independent), NY (villages dependent, cities independent), MI (villages dependent, cities independent), MN (a handful of dependent cities, the rest independent). The belief in the brief is confirmed: WI villages and cities and nearly all MN cities are independent, so "kept as cells" there is the same situation as NJ/PA boroughs. The real asymmetry in the project is only VT villages + CT boroughs/Groton city (folded) versus NY villages + MI villages + the few MN dependent cities (cut out); Census puts all of these in the same "dependent" class.

### Cited Findings

General rule
- "In nine states—Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Jersey, North Dakota, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Dakota, and Wisconsin—all incorporated places are independent places. In other states, incorporated places are part of, or dependent within, the MCDs in which they are located, or the pattern is mixed—some incorporated places are independent of MCDs and others are included within one or more MCDs." (p. A-21) — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- "Dependent places are part of the county subdivision; the county subdivision code of the place is the same as that of the underlying county subdivision(s), but is different from the place code. Independent places are not part of any minor civil division (MCD) and serve as primary county subdivisions." (p. A-29) — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- GARM definition of independence is governmental, not just cartographic: independent incorporated places are "incorporated places with governments that function independently from the jurisdiction of the surrounding MCD or MCDs. They do not receive governmental services from any MCD, except when they undertake to contract for such services. Their inhabitants do not pay taxes to any MCD, nor do they vote in any MCD elections." (pp. 8-15 to 8-16) — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Dependent place, per Figure 8-3 caption: "the incorporated place is governmentally subordinate to, or dependent upon, the MCDs in which it is located." (p. 8-32) — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- PEP uses the same word: "In some states, all or some incorporated places are subordinate to the MCDs in which they are located. Therefore, a place may be either independent of or dependent within MCDs." — [PEP terms](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/guidance-geographies/terms-and-definitions.html)

### Inferences
- Within the twelve states, the set of dependent incorporated places is: CT 30 (of which 20 coextensive with their town, so only 10 matter: 8 boroughs, Woodmont borough, Groton city), VT 29 active villages, NY 533 villages (+ Sherrill city), MI 253 villages, MN 4 cities. By count, the project's "fold" rule touches about 39 places and its "cut out" rule touches about 790 dependent places of exactly the same Census class.
- In the eight "fold" states the rule is a no-op in ME, MA, NH, RI, NJ, PA (no dependent places exist). In the four "cut out" states the rule is a no-op in WI and almost so in MN (places are already their own county subdivisions). The asymmetry is therefore entirely CT+VT versus NY+MI (plus 4 MN cities).
- Census gives no class distinction between a VT village, a CT borough, a NY village and a MI village: all are "dependent within county subdivision", all are "governmentally subordinate to" the MCD in GARM's wording.

### Gaps
- No 2020-vintage "Guide to State and Local Census Geography" exists at the analogous URL (https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/2020/geo/state-local-geo-guides-2020.html returned HTTP 404); the 2010 guides are the latest found.
- MN: the drop from 9 dependent cities (2010 guide) to 4 in SUB-EST2024 was not traced to boundary change notes. 57 MN place records are carried at SUMLEV 157 with PRIMGEO_FLAG=1 (e.g. Hennepin County cities), i.e. in counties where PEP shows no MCD level, so the SUB-EST count of dependent MN cities may understate the TIGER count. Verify against TIGER COUSUB/PLACE class codes (C1 vs C5) if MN matters.
- MI: one zero-population record "St. Louis city (pt.)" appears as a dependent part inside a township (county 057, COUSUB 08020); not investigated.
- NY: Sherrill city's parent town name was not confirmed from Census text (only the COUSUB code 77123 was seen).

## 2. What exactly does the 2020 PL 94-171 technical documentation Appendix A (pp. A-21 to A-22) say about the twelve states?

### Takeaway
Appendix A names the twelve states and says their MCDs "serve as general-purpose local governments that can perform the same governmental functions as incorporated places" and get data in every product that has place data. It treats the twelve as one group, with no sub-grouping of New England versus NY/MI/MN/WI, and it says nothing about folding dependent places into MCDs.

### Cited Findings
- Verbatim, spanning the page break A-21/A-22: "The MCDs in 12 states (Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Wisconsin) also serve as general-purpose local governments that can perform the same governmental functions as incorporated places. The Census Bureau presents data for these MCDs in all data products for which place data are provided." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- Immediately preceding paragraph (p. A-21): "In some states, all or some incorporated places are not part of any MCD; these places are termed independent places. Independent places also serve as primary legal subdivisions and have a Federal Information Processing Series (FIPS) county subdivision code and National Standard (NS) code that is the same as the FIPS and NS place code." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- MCD definition (p. A-21): "Minor civil divisions (MCDs) are the primary governmental or administrative divisions of a county in many states... The Census Bureau recognizes MCDs in 29 states, Puerto Rico, and the Island Areas." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- Place definition (p. A-28): "An incorporated place is established to provide governmental functions for a concentration of people as opposed to a minor civil division (MCD), which generally is created to provide services or administer an area without regard, necessarily, to population... For Census Bureau data tabulation and presentation purposes, incorporated places exclude: • Boroughs in Alaska (treated as statistical equivalents of counties). • Towns in the New England states, New York, and Wisconsin (treated as MCDs). • Boroughs in New York (treated as MCDs)." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- Dependent/independent paragraph (p. A-29), remainder: "The independent place Federal Information Processing Series (FIPS) code usually is the same as that used for the MCD for that place. The only exception is if the place is independent of the MCDs in a state (Iowa, Louisiana, Maryland, Nebraska, North Carolina, and Virginia) in which the FIPS MCD codes are in the 90000 range... Census designated places (CDPs) always are dependent within county subdivisions and all places are dependent within statistical county subdivisions." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- Class codes (data dictionary, pdf pp. 78-80): place "C1 An active incorporated place that does not serve as a county subdivision equivalent"; "C2 An active incorporated place that is legally coextensive with a county subdivision but treated as independent of any county subdivision (an independent place)"; "C5 An active incorporated place that is independent of any county subdivision and serves as a county subdivision equivalent (an independent place)"; "C6 ... partially independent ... and partially dependent ... (exists in Iowa and Ohio only)"; county subdivision "T1 An active county subdivision that is not coextensive with an incorporated place"; "T5 An active county subdivision that is coextensive with an incorporated place". — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- Functional status codes (p. A-23) include "C Active government consolidated with another government with a single set of officials", "F Fictitious entity created to fill the Census Bureau's geographic hierarchy", "G Active government that is subordinate to another unit of government and thus, not considered a functioning government." — [PL-TD](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/summary-file/2020Census_PL94_171Redistricting_StatesTechDoc_English.pdf)
- PEP repeats the twelve-state list and adds the contrast with eight weaker states: "Most of the MCDs in twelve states (Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Wisconsin) serve as general-purpose local governments. In the remaining eight states where the Census Bureau produces MCD level estimates (Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, and South Dakota) the MCDs, for the most part, perform less of a governmental role and are less well known locally, even though they are active governmental units." — [PEP terms](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/guidance-geographies/terms-and-definitions.html)

### Inferences
- The twelve-state passage is a statement about MCDs being place-like, not about places being MCD-subordinate. It supports "the town is a locality" in all twelve states equally; it does not by itself tell you what to do with a dependent village.
- Dependent villages and boroughs in VT, CT, NY, MI all carry functional status "A" in SUB-EST2024 (see section 5), not "G" (subordinate, not a functioning government). In Census coding, "dependent" is a geographic-nesting attribute, and the village is still an active general-purpose government.

### Gaps
- The exact phrase "treated like places" does not appear on pp. A-21 to A-22; the operative wording is the two sentences quoted above.

## 3. What do GARM chapters 8 and 9 say about dependent places, and about New England villages/boroughs specifically?

### Takeaway
GARM (1994) explains dependent places with a figure and tabulation rule (place data are a subtotal of the MCD), and has one paragraph on New England: all CT places are dependent, VT cities independent and VT villages dependent. It describes NY villages in the same terms one page later. It does distinguish New England from the Middle Atlantic on strength of town identity, and the Northeast from the Midwest on township strength, but never says a New England village is more subordinate than a NY or MI one.

### Cited Findings
- New England paragraph, verbatim (p. 8-21): "Relationship of towns to incorporated places in New England All incorporated places in Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Rhode Island are cities that are independent of any town. All incorporated places (cities and boroughs) in Connecticut are dependent on the town in which they are located. One borough and all but one of the State's twenty cities are coextensive with a single town, and exercise the governmental powers of both an MCD and an incorporated place in a single elected governmental body. The incorporated places in Vermont are either cities, all of which are independent of MCDs, or villages, all of which are dependent. Unlike Connecticut, none of the dependent villages in Vermont coincide with a town." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Middle Atlantic paragraph (p. 8-22): "In New Jersey and Pennsylvania, all incorporated places are independent of townships and form primary subdivisions of their counties... The relationship between incorporated places and MCDs in New York is slightly different—all incorporated cities are independent of any MCD, but all incorporated villages are dependent on the towns in which they are located... Elsewhere in the State, five villages each are coextensive with a single town (see Table 8-4). In three of these villages (East Rochester, Mount Kisco, and Scarsdale), the residents elect a single set of government officials to perform the functions of both the town and the village." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- New England vs Middle Atlantic towns (pp. 8-21 to 8-22): Middle Atlantic MCDs "share some of the legal and geographic attributes of the New England towns in that they all are significant, active, functioning governmental units... However, there are two major differences: (1) counties in the Middle Atlantic States have greater governmental and administrative significance than in New England, and (2) the local inhabitants do not always perceive the MCD as constituting a single community. An illustration of the somewhat weaker community identification of MCDs in the Middle Atlantic States is the large number of separately incorporated places (nearly 2,000) and CDPs (about 800) in these three States." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Northeast as a whole (p. 8-20): "In the New England and Middle Atlantic States, the primary subdivisions of counties generally are called towns or townships. Most of these towns and townships are actively functioning units of local government and are very well known locally. Although not classified as incorporated places in the decennial census, they are legally incorporated units and have most or all the powers of incorporated places. Because of this strong functional aspect, the Census Bureau usually provides the same data tables for these MCDs as it provides for places, and also tables where MCDs and places are intermixed." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- New England towns (pp. 8-20 to 8-21): "the New England towns were established initially to provide government to an area rather than a specific concentration of population... In New England, the towns and cities, not the county, serve as the basic units of local government." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Midwest (p. 8-23): "These MCDs, for the most part, perform less of a governmental role and are less well known locally than their counterparts in the Northeast and the Middle Atlantic States. There are exceptions—the charter townships of Michigan, the urban townships of Minnesota, and the towns of Wisconsin—all of which have the legal capacity to provide all the governmental services associated with incorporated places." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Tabulation rule (p. 8-29): "In the hierarchical data tables, dependent places are indented under the name of the MCD/CCD in which they are located, and the count for each dependent place is included in that MCD/CCD total." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Figure 8-3 (p. 8-32), dependent case: "the data for each MCD include the data for every incorporated place and every part of an incorporated place it contains... The MCDs include the appropriate portion of the data for the contained incorporated place as a subtotal of the MCD total. With this type of governmental structure, changes in the boundaries of the incorporated place do not change the boundaries of, or the data for, the MCDs." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Figure 8-2 (p. 8-31), independent case: "the MCDs stop at the limits of the incorporated place... The data for the MCDs exclude the data for the incorporated place they border... any change in the boundaries of the incorporated place will change both the territory of, and the data for, the adjacent MCDs. The Census Bureau treats the incorporated place both as a pseudo MCD and as a place in its data tabulations." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- Chapter 9 uses Vermont as its example of the mixed pattern (p. 9-10): "All incorporated places within a State may be independent of any MCD (as in Wisconsin), dependent within an MCD (as in Mississippi), or there may be a mixture of independent and dependent incorporated places (for example, in Vermont, villages are dependent within MCDs, while cities are independent of any MCD). Dependent places frequently are located in more than one MCD." — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)
- Chapter 9 on independent places (p. 9-10): they "stand alone in the Census Bureau's statistical presentations; that is, they appear in the same alphabetical format within counties as the MCDs, and their statistical information is not included in that of another entity except the county." — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)
- Incorporation requirements, Table 9-1: Connecticut city "Incorporation is by special act of the State legislature; there is no minimum population requirement"; borough "Same requirements as for a city" (p. 9-3). Vermont city by special act; "Village Must contain 30 or more houses." (p. 9-7) — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)
- CT governmental status, Table 8-3 (p. 8-12): "Of the 169 towns, 149 are actively functioning governmental units. The remaining 20 towns are areally coextensive with a single incorporated place; 19 towns are coextensive with a city and one town is coextensive with a borough. In each of these 20 instances, the town and city governments are consolidated, and the Census Bureau classifies the incorporated place government as the active government." — [GARM 8](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch8GARM.pdf)
- 1990 incorporated place counts (ch. 9 table, pp. 9-30 to 9-31): Connecticut 31, Vermont 51, New York 619, Michigan 534, Minnesota 854, Wisconsin 583. — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)

### Inferences
- GARM's only gradient that could justify different handling is about the MCD, not the village: New England towns have the strongest community identity; NY/NJ/PA towns/townships are "significant, active" but with "somewhat weaker community identification"; Midwestern townships are weaker again, with MI charter townships, MN urban townships and WI towns as exceptions. That gradient is an argument about how much the town matters as a locality, and it is a matter of degree across NY and MI, not a categorical line between VT/CT and NY/MI.
- GARM's Figure 8-3 logic (dependent place = subtotal of the MCD; MCD boundary unaffected by village boundary) is the direct Census precedent for folding. It applies identically to NY and MI villages. Applying it only in VT/CT is not something GARM supports.
- GARM's Figure 8-2 logic (independent place = its own county subdivision) is the precedent for cells in WI, NJ, PA and for VT/NY/MI cities; it does not cover NY/MI villages.

### Gaps
- GARM is 1994 (1990 census vintage). No later edition exists; current wording comes from PL-TD and the 2010 guides.
- GARM has no passage specific to CT boroughs beyond the lines quoted; nothing on why CT places are all dependent.

## 5. How do Census data products handle a dependent place and its parent town? Does any product treat VT/CT dependent places differently from NY/MI ones?

### Takeaway
No product examined treats them differently. In the decennial hierarchy the village is a subtotal of the town; in the Population Estimates subcounty file VT, CT, NY, MI and MN all get the same structure: a whole-town record (061), a village part record (071) and a "Balance of [town]" record (071), with the part and the balance flagged as primitive geography. ## 6. Does New England-specific Census practice give a documented reason to treat a VT village or CT borough as subordinate to its town in a way a NY village is not?

### Takeaway
No. Every "towns treated like places" practice found is defined for a group that includes New York (and usually NJ, PA, MI, WI, MN), not New England alone. The one genuinely New England-only construct, NECTAs, was built from towns and cities and has been discontinued by OMB under the 2020 standards. What the documents do support is a difference of degree in how strongly the town is the community (New England strongest, Middle Atlantic "somewhat weaker", Midwest weaker with exceptions), which is an argument about towns, not a rule about villages.

### Cited Findings
- 1990 practice, twelve states together (p. 9-24): "In its 1990 data presentations, the Census Bureau has included the MCDs in 12 States (the 6 New England States plus Michigan, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin) in some of the data products that present tabulations for places of 2,500 or more. The MCDs in these States serve as general purpose local governments, and they possess legal or governmental powers similar to those of incorporated places." — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)
- Historical whole-town treatment was not New England-only (p. 9-24): "The 1980 census included a whole-town CDP category, whereby MCDs in New England, the Middle Atlantic States, Michigan, and Wisconsin were treated as places (for urban/rural qualification and whole-count purposes) if 95 percent or more of their population and 80 percent or more of their land area qualified for inclusion in a UA. The Census Bureau long has grappled with the proper treatment of these strong governmental entities, particularly in the classification of their populations as urban or rural. These MCDs also were treated as places in the 1940, 1960, and some earlier censuses. The Census Bureau dropped the whole-town CDP category for the 1990 census". — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)
- The historical rules excluded towns that contained incorporated places (Table 9-6, p. 9-25): 1960 "Urban towns in New England, and urban townships in New Jersey and Pennsylvania qualified as urban places if they had no incorporated places, and either (1) 25,000 or more inhabitants or (2) 2,500 to 24,999 inhabitants and a density of at least 1,500 people per square mile."; 1980 "Towns in New England, New York, and Wisconsin, and townships in New Jersey and Pennsylvania qualified as urban places (also called whole-town CDPs) if they had no incorporated places, 1,000 or more inhabitants, and if both 90 percent of the population and 80 percent of the land area met the minimum density requirement for inclusion in a UA." — [GARM 9](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch9GARM.pdf)

### Inferences
- The historical whole-town rules treated a town as a place only "if they had no incorporated places". Where a village or borough existed, Census kept the incorporated place as the place and did not promote the town. That is evidence against a Census tradition of subsuming VT villages or CT boroughs into the town for place purposes.
- NECTA building blocks were county subdivisions (towns and independent cities); a dependent village or borough was never a NECTA unit. That is a New England-only practice in which the town, not the village, was the unit, but it is a metropolitan-area convention, defunct since the 2020 standards, and says nothing comparable about NY because NY simply had no town-based metro areas.
- The defensible documentary argument for an asymmetry would be the GARM gradient of town identity (New England > Middle Atlantic > Midwest), plus scale: VT villages and the 8 separate CT boroughs are small and few (see section 7), while NY and MI villages number in the hundreds. Census itself draws no such line; it would be a project policy choice, consistent with the project memory note that the strong-town rule was "chosen for policy, not accuracy".
- A single rule is better supported by Census documentation than the current split: either fold all dependent places in the twelve states (060 logic) or cut all of them out (PEP 071 logic).

### Gaps
- The Federal Register notice and ACS page were not read in full; the NECTA statements rest on a search summary and should be confirmed before quoting.
- Did not locate historical printed New England reports (e.g. 1990 CPH tables) to check how villages were listed under towns; relied on GARM's description.
- No Census document was found that discusses village-versus-town primacy specifically for Vermont or Connecticut. Absence was established only across the sources listed at the top.

### Inferences
- The one inactive VT village in the gazetteer (FUNCSTAT I) is almost certainly Jericho: the 2010 guide names it as inactive, it is in the 2025 gazetteer list, and it is the only village absent from SUB-EST2024. A fold rule and a cut-out rule should both ignore it.
- Perkinsville's deletion is dated 1 July 2020, after the 1 January 2020 census boundary date, so it exists in 2020 census geography but not in vintages from 2021 on. Any pipeline mixing 2020 census tables with current TIGER will see 33 vs 30 villages.
- Scale of the decision: folding VT villages and the separate CT places moves about 23,600 people (VT) and about 23,800 people (CT: 8 boroughs 12,974 + Woodmont 1,471 + Groton city 9,386) from would-be village cells into towns. Cutting out NY and MI villages creates about 786 cells holding about 2.2 million people. A uniform fold rule would be the large change (NY villages include places of nearly 60,000); a uniform cut-out rule would add only about 39 small cells in VT/CT.
- Essex Junction no longer needs special handling: as of the 2023 BAS it is an independent city and its own county subdivision.

### Gaps
- 2020 census populations (P1 counts) for the 33 villages as tabulated in 2020, including Lyndonville and Perkinsville, were not retrieved (the Census API returned an empty response without a key). Figures above are the PEP 2020 estimates base, which can differ slightly from published census counts (e.g. Essex Junction 10,603 base vs 10,590 census count reported by Wikipedia, unverified).
- Which annual GCN file contains each 2021-2025 note was not recorded; all were read from the national_gcn_2021 to national_gcn_2025 files linked from the GCN page.
- VT 2011-2013 change notes and NY/MI village dissolutions were not reviewed systematically.
- No "CT borough consolidation" event was found in 2014-2025 notes; whether any is pending in 2026 BAS was not checked. TIGER 2026 was not checked; the latest vintage examined is the 2025 gazetteer.
